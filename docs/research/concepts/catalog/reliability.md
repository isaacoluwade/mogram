# Reliability & resilience: concept catalog

Stage 1 research for the Reliability area. Every major concept, checked against the Google SRE
books, the AWS and Azure Well-Architected reliability guidance, the AWS disaster recovery
whitepaper, the Amazon Builders' Library, _Release It!_ and the Principles of Chaos Engineering.

## Scope

**In:** how systems stay up and come back when parts of them break.

- **Foundations:** the vocabulary (availability, reliability, durability, resilience), the
  nines and availability arithmetic, single points of failure, redundancy levels, fault
  domains, correlated failure, blast radius, failure mode analysis.
- **How things fail:** failure modes (crash, omission, slow, wrong), partial failure, gray
  failure, fail-slow, and how failure spreads: cascading failure, overload, retry storms,
  thundering herds, metastable failures, poison pills.
- **Resilience patterns** for calls between services: timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter,
  idempotency keys, circuit breakers, bulkheads, fail fast, deadlines, hedging, load shedding,
  health checks, self-healing.
- **Fault isolation architecture:** cells, shuffle sharding, static stability, control plane vs
  data plane, constant work.
- **Dependencies and degradation:** critical vs non-critical dependencies, graceful degradation,
  fallback vs failover, kill switches.
- **Zones, regions and failover:** multi-AZ, multi-region, active-active vs active-passive,
  failover (Mogram already ships `failover`), failback, DNS failover, evacuation, and the
  coordination problems underneath (heartbeats, split brain, leader election).
- **SRE practice:** SLI/SLO/SLA, error budgets and burn rate, MTTx metrics, incidents, on-call,
  postmortems, runbooks, toil, chaos engineering, fault injection, game days.
- **Disaster recovery:** RPO vs RTO, HA vs DR, the four DR strategies, replication vs backup,
  DR testing.

**Left to other areas** (details in [Overlaps](#overlaps)):

- **Fundamentals:** what regions and availability zones are; service quotas.
- **Networking:** load balancing, DNS resolution and TTLs, anycast, global load balancers,
  connection draining, redundant hybrid connectivity.
- **Containers:** Kubernetes liveness/readiness/startup probes, PodDisruptionBudgets, topology
  spread, graceful pod shutdown.
- **Data:** replication (sync vs async), quorum, consensus (Raft/Paxos), backup types and
  point-in-time restore, multi-region write conflicts.
- **Messaging:** idempotent consumers, dead-letter queues, sagas and compensating transactions,
  queue-based load levelling, backpressure.
- **Scaling:** rate limiting and throttling, autoscaling, capacity planning, cache stampede,
  tail latency, load testing.
- **Delivery:** canary, rolling and blue-green deployments, feature flags, automated rollback,
  infrastructure as code.
- **Observability:** golden signals, alerting (including multiwindow burn-rate SLO alerts),
  health models, synthetic monitoring.
- **Architecture:** deployment stamps and geodes (same ideas as cells and active-active
  multi-region), loose coupling, stateless services.
- **Platform files:** every named service (Route 53 ARC, zonal shift, Azure availability sets,
  Site Recovery, Chaos Studio, AWS FIS, and so on).

### 1. Foundations: vocabulary and availability math

| id                                  | Concept                                    | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                               | Often confused with                                                      |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ---- | -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| availability-reliability-resilience | Availability vs reliability vs resilience  | 1    | any      | Availability: the share of time (or of requests) a service is usable. Reliability: it does the right thing, correctly and consistently, over time. Resilience: it withstands faults and recovers from them.                                                | A service can be "up" yet wrong or too slow to use; resilience is the property that keeps availability and reliability high while parts are breaking.                                | Durability; high availability                                            |
| durability-vs-availability          | Durability vs availability                 | 1    | any      | Durability is the chance stored data is never lost; availability is the chance you can reach it right now. Durability comes from keeping copies (a replication factor of 3, or erasure coding) across independent failure domains.                         | Data can be perfectly safe and still unreachable during an outage, and a highly available store can still lose data. "Eleven nines of durability" says nothing about uptime.         | Availability; backup                                                     |
| nines-of-availability               | The nines and downtime budgets             | 1    | any      | Availability as a percentage, where each extra "nine" cuts allowed downtime tenfold: 99.9% is about 8.8 hours a year (43 minutes a month), 99.99% about 53 minutes a year, 99.999% about 5 minutes a year.                                                 | Each nine allows ten times less downtime and usually costs far more. At four or five nines there isn't time for a person to respond, so recovery has to be automatic.                | SLA (the nines are a measure; an SLA is a promise)                       |
| composite-availability              | Composite availability: serial vs parallel | 2    | any      | How availabilities combine. Dependencies in series multiply (three 99.9% parts in a chain give about 99.7%); redundant copies in parallel multiply their chances of failing (two 99% copies give 99.99%).                                                  | Every hard dependency lowers your ceiling below its own availability; redundancy is the only arithmetic that raises it, and only when the copies fail independently.                 | Averaging the SLAs                                                       |
| single-point-of-failure             | Single point of failure (SPOF)             | 1    | any      | Any component whose failure alone stops the whole service: one server, one database, one load balancer, one DNS provider, one certificate, one person with the password.                                                                                   | Trace a request end to end and ask "if only this dies, does everything stop?" The SPOF is often the thing nobody drew, like a shared config store.                                   | Bottleneck                                                               |
| redundancy-n-plus-1                 | Redundancy: N, N+1, N+2, 2N                | 1    | any      | How much spare capacity is kept: N is exactly enough; N+1 survives one failure; N+2 survives a failure during maintenance; 2N duplicates everything.                                                                                                       | Redundancy only counts if the survivors can carry the load: two servers each at 60% can't survive losing one, because the other would need to run at 120%.                           | Scaling out for performance                                              |
| correlated-failure                  | Correlated failures                        | 2    | any      | Failures that hit several "redundant" copies at once because they share something: power, a rack, a zone, a deployment, a config push, a bug, a certificate expiry, a dependency.                                                                          | Redundancy assumes the copies fail independently. Three replicas with the same bad config are one replica, so spread copies across fault domains and change them one at a time.      | Single point of failure                                                  |
| fault-domains                       | Fault domains and update domains           | 2    | any      | Groups of hardware that fail together (host, rack, power feed, switch, zone, region) and groups that are taken down together for planned maintenance.                                                                                                      | Place copies so no single rack, zone or maintenance batch holds all of them; the size of the domain you spread across decides which failures you survive.                            | Availability zones (one kind of fault domain)                            |
| blast-radius                        | Blast radius                               | 1    | any      | How far the impact of a failure or a bad change reaches: which users, tenants, services or regions are hit.                                                                                                                                                | You can't prevent every failure, but you can choose how far each one spreads; zones, cells, bulkheads and staged rollouts all exist to shrink this number.                           | Severity                                                                 |
| failure-mode-analysis               | Failure mode analysis (FMA)                | 2    | any      | A design exercise: walk each critical user flow component by component, list how each can fail (crash, overload, misconfiguration, zone or region loss, dependency outage), how likely that is, what users would see, and how it's detected and mitigated. | Reliability design starts from "how will this break, and what will users see?", not from adding replicas everywhere; the output is a ranked list of risks to design for and to test. | Postmortem (after the fact); chaos engineering (tests the FMA's guesses) |

### 2. How things fail

| id                   | Concept                                     | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                            | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                           | Often confused with                |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| failure-modes        | Failure modes: crash, omission, slow, wrong | 2    | any      | The ways a component fails, roughly from easiest to hardest to detect: it crashes and stops (fail-stop), it drops some requests, it answers too slowly, or it answers wrongly (corrupt or byzantine). | A crash is the friendly failure: obvious, and quick to route around. The dangerous ones keep answering, so good designs turn them into crashes (fail fast, crash-only software). | Outage (one outcome of many modes) |
| partial-failure      | Partial failure                             | 2    | any      | In a distributed system some parts fail while others keep working, and a single network call can fail at any step: sending, delivery, processing, the reply, its delivery.                            | A timed-out call has a third outcome besides success and failure: unknown. You can't tell whether the work happened, which is why retries need idempotency.                      | Total outage                       |
| gray-failure         | Gray failure                                | 3    | any      | A failure that health checks don't see but users do ("differential observability"): a host that passes its health check but drops 5% of requests, or a zone that is slow only for some traffic.       | Your monitoring and your users can disagree. Detect gray failure from the client's side (error rate per host or per zone) and act on that, for example by draining the zone.     | Partial failure; crash             |
| slow-is-the-new-down | Fail-slow: slow is the new down             | 2    | any      | A dependency that responds very slowly instead of failing: a degraded disk, a saturated database, a congested link.                                                                                   | A dead dependency fails fast; a slow one holds your threads, connections and memory until you run out and go down too. Slowness spreads failure further than crashes do.         | Normal latency variation           |

### 3. Overload and how failure spreads

| id                   | Concept                              | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Often confused with                                     |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| cascading-failure    | Cascading failure                    | 2    | any      | A failure that spreads: one server dies, its load moves to the others, they overload and die in turn; or a slow database backs up every service that calls it.                                                                | Failure travels along load and dependencies. The fixes are the ones that stop propagation: headroom, timeouts, load shedding, circuit breakers and bulkheads.                                                                 | Correlated failure (simultaneous rather than spreading) |
| overload-and-goodput | Overload and goodput collapse        | 2    | any      | What happens past capacity: queues grow, latency rises, clients time out and give up, yet the server keeps working on requests nobody is waiting for. Throughput stays high while useful work (goodput) falls toward zero.    | An overloaded server that accepts everything serves almost nobody; refusing some work early is how it keeps serving the rest.                                                                                                 | Throughput                                              |
| retry-storm          | Retry storms and retry amplification | 2    | any      | Retries adding load to a service that is failing because of load. Retries at every layer multiply: three tries at each of five layers can become 243 calls at the bottom.                                                     | Retries help with rare, random failures and hurt during overload. Cap them with a retry budget (for example, retries at most 10% of traffic), retry at one layer only, and back off when the server says it's overloaded.     | Thundering herd                                         |
| thundering-herd      | Thundering herd                      | 2    | any      | Many clients acting at the same moment: reconnecting after an outage, refreshing the same expired cache key, running a job at midnight, retrying on the same schedule.                                                        | Synchronisation is the enemy: the same load arriving in one second can flatten a service that handles it easily over a minute. Add jitter, stagger schedules, coalesce identical requests, warm caches before taking traffic. | Retry storm; organic traffic spike                      |
| metastable-failure   | Metastable failure                   | 3    | any      | A system knocked into a bad state by a trigger (a load spike, a cache flush, a brief outage) that stays bad after the trigger is gone, because a feedback loop such as retries or cache misses keeps the load above capacity. | Removing the trigger doesn't end the outage. The system handled the same load fine before; you have to break the loop (shed load, stop retries, warm the cache) to get back.                                                  | Cascading failure                                       |
| poison-pill          | Poison pill (query of death)         | 3    | any      | A request or message that crashes or hangs whatever processes it. Because it is retried, it moves from server to server, or blocks the head of a queue, taking each down in turn.                                             | The input that kills one replica kills them all, so redundancy doesn't help. Limit retries, detect and quarantine the input (a dead-letter queue for messages), and keep it from reaching every replica.                      | Bad deploy; load spike                                  |

### 4. Resilience patterns for calls between services

| id                            | Concept                                     | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Often confused with                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| timeouts                      | Timeouts                                    | 1    | any      | A limit on how long a caller waits for a dependency (to connect, and for the whole request) before giving up, usually set from the dependency's measured latency, a little above its p99.9.                                                          | Without a timeout, one slow dependency can hold every thread you have; with one, slowness becomes a quick error you can handle. A timeout doesn't mean the work didn't happen.                                              | Deadline propagation                            |
| retry-backoff-jitter          | Retries with exponential backoff and jitter | 1    | any      | Retrying transient failures after waits that double each time (100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms, up to a cap), with a random spread (jitter) so clients don't retry in lockstep. Only transient errors are retried; bad requests aren't.                       | Backoff gives a struggling service room to recover; jitter turns synchronised waves of retries into a steady trickle. Retrying without either can make an outage worse.                                                     | Circuit breaker                                 |
| idempotency-key               | Idempotency keys for safe retries           | 2    | any      | A unique token the client sends with a request that changes something; the server records the result against the token and returns the same result if the request comes again, instead of doing the work twice.                                      | Retries are only safe when doing something twice has the same effect as doing it once. An idempotency key turns "charge the card" into something you can retry after a timeout.                                             | Idempotent consumer (messaging); deduplication  |
| circuit-breaker               | Circuit breaker                             | 1    | any      | A wrapper around calls to a dependency that trips after too many failures. Closed: calls flow. Open: calls fail immediately for a cool-down. Half-open: a few trial calls go through; success closes it, failure opens it again.                     | Stop hammering something that is down. Failing fast saves your own threads and gives the dependency room to recover, and the half-open state lets traffic return by itself.                                                 | Retry; bulkhead                                 |
| bulkhead                      | Bulkhead                                    | 2    | any      | Separate pools of resources (threads, connections, instances, queues) for different dependencies, features or customers, so one can't drain the others. Named after a ship's watertight compartments.                                                | A failure should flood one compartment, not sink the ship: if the slow recommendations call can use only its own ten threads, checkout keeps its threads.                                                                   | Circuit breaker; cell-based architecture        |
| fail-fast                     | Fail fast and bounded queues                | 2    | any      | Rejecting work immediately when it can't succeed: a required dependency is known to be down, a resource is missing, or the queue in front of the workers is already full. Queues get a maximum length instead of growing without limit.              | A request that will fail should fail in a millisecond, not after holding resources for 30 seconds. An unbounded queue turns overload into latency, then into running out of memory.                                         | Load shedding                                   |
| deadline-propagation          | Deadline propagation                        | 3    | any      | Passing the remaining time budget down a call chain (for example gRPC deadlines) so every service knows how long the original caller will still wait, and cancelling work when the time runs out.                                                    | Without it, services deep in the chain keep working on requests the user abandoned long ago, wasting capacity exactly when it's scarce.                                                                                     | Timeouts (local to each hop)                    |
| hedged-requests               | Hedged requests                             | 3    | any      | Sending a duplicate request to a second replica if the first hasn't answered within a typical latency (for example the 95th percentile), using whichever answer arrives first and cancelling the other.                                              | About 5% extra load buys a large cut in tail latency, because one slow replica no longer decides how slow the request is. Only safe for idempotent calls.                                                                   | Retry (waits for a failure; hedging doesn't)    |
| load-shedding                 | Load shedding                               | 2    | any      | A server deliberately rejecting excess requests with a cheap, fast error (such as 503) as it nears capacity, dropping the least important traffic first.                                                                                             | Serving 80% of requests well beats serving 100% badly. Shed early and cheaply, before work has been spent on the request, so the server keeps its goodput.                                                                  | Rate limiting (a per-client policy); throttling |
| shallow-vs-deep-health-checks | Health checks: shallow vs deep              | 2    | any      | Endpoints that report whether an instance should receive traffic (the Health Endpoint Monitoring pattern). Shallow checks only test that the process answers; deep checks also test its dependencies (database, disk, downstream services).          | Shallow checks miss broken instances; deep checks can fail every instance at once when a shared dependency blips, pulling the whole fleet out of service. Check what's local, and treat shared dependencies differently.    | Liveness vs readiness probes (containers)       |
| health-check-fail-open        | Failing open on health checks               | 3    | any      | A load balancer rule: if every target looks unhealthy, send traffic to all of them rather than none. Its companion: automation that removes or replaces unhealthy hosts is capped so it can't take out more than a small share of the fleet at once. | When everything fails a check at the same moment, the check is more likely wrong than the fleet. Failing open, and limiting automated removal, stop a bad health check from causing the outage.                             | Fail fast                                       |
| self-healing                  | Self-healing and automated recovery         | 2    | any      | Detecting a failure and fixing it without a person: restarting a crashed process, replacing an unhealthy instance, failing over a database, moving work off a bad host. Instances are disposable and replaced rather than repaired.                  | Recovery that waits for a person takes minutes to hours; recovery built into the platform takes seconds, and that is where high availability actually comes from. Give the automation limits so it can't make things worse. | Autoscaling (responds to load, not failure)     |

### 5. Fault isolation architecture

| id                          | Concept                                 | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                           | Often confused with                   |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| cell-based-architecture     | Cell-based architecture                 | 3    | any      | Splitting a service into many complete, independent copies (cells), each serving a fixed subset of customers behind a thin routing layer. Cells share nothing and have a maximum size. Also called deployment stamps or scale units.                                                  | A bug, bad deploy or overload takes out one cell's customers instead of everyone. The routing layer must stay simpler and more reliable than anything it routes to.              | Sharding (data only); multi-region    |
| shuffle-sharding            | Shuffle sharding                        | 3    | any      | Giving each customer a random combination of a few workers out of many: with 8 workers and 2 per customer there are 28 different pairs. A customer whose traffic kills its workers shares both of them with only a small fraction of other customers.                                 | Plain sharding puts a noisy or poisonous customer's neighbours in the same failure; shuffle sharding makes it very unlikely that any other customer loses all of its workers.    | Sharding; cells                       |
| static-stability            | Static stability                        | 2    | any      | Designing so the system keeps working through a failure without changing anything: capacity for losing a zone is already running (three zones, each sized for 50% of peak), and data and config are already where they're needed. Also means quota headroom in the failover location. | Recovery that has to launch instances, call APIs or change DNS during the failure depends on things that may be failing too. Pre-provision, so nothing has to happen.            | Autoscaling; failover                 |
| control-plane-vs-data-plane | Control plane vs data plane in recovery | 3    | any      | The control plane creates and configures resources (launch an instance, change a route or a DNS record); the data plane does the ongoing work (serves traffic, answers DNS queries). Data planes are simpler and usually more available.                                              | Plan recovery so it uses only data-plane actions. A failover that needs a control-plane API call can fail exactly when the provider is having a bad day.                         | Management plane                      |
| constant-work               | Constant work                           | 3    | any      | A design where a system does the same amount of work whatever is happening, for example pushing the full configuration every few seconds instead of only the changes, or checking every target every cycle.                                                                           | A system that does the same work in a crisis as on a quiet day can't be overloaded by the crisis. Sudden bursts of change, not steady load, are what break many control systems. | Polling (inefficient on purpose here) |

### 6. Dependencies and degradation

| id                                   | Concept                               | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                         | Often confused with                    |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| critical-vs-noncritical-dependencies | Critical vs non-critical dependencies | 2    | any      | Classifying each dependency of a user flow as hard (the flow fails without it, like payments for checkout) or soft (the flow can finish without it, like recommendations or reviews). Critical flows get the strictest targets. | Many outages spread through dependencies that should have been soft. Give the flow a way to finish without a dependency and your availability stops depending on its availability.             | Composite availability                 |
| graceful-degradation                 | Graceful degradation                  | 1    | any      | Keeping the core of a service working when parts fail, by turning off or simplifying non-essential features: serve cached or static results, hide recommendations, go read-only.                                                | It isn't "all up or all down". Decide in advance what the reduced service looks like; users notice a missing sidebar far less than an error page.                                              | Failover; fallback                     |
| fallback-vs-failover                 | Fallback vs failover                  | 3    | any      | Failover moves work to another copy of the same thing; fallback switches to a different mechanism (a local cache, a simpler algorithm, a second provider) that only runs during a failure.                                      | Fallback code runs rarely, so it's the least-tested code you own and can make things worse when it finally runs. Prefer failover, alternatives that are always on, or a more reliable primary. | Graceful degradation                   |
| kill-switch                          | Kill switches (emergency levers)      | 2    | any      | Pre-built, tested switches that turn off a feature, a dependency call or a class of traffic in seconds, without a deploy, usually through feature flags or runtime config.                                                      | Mid-incident there's no time to write and ship code; a lever that already exists turns a crisis into a toggle. Test the levers like any other path.                                            | Feature flags for releasing (delivery) |

### 7. Zones, regions and failover

| multi-az-deployment             | Multi-AZ deployment             | 1    | any      | Running copies of each tier in two or more availability zones of a region (separate data centres with their own power and network, close enough for synchronous replication) behind a load balancer.          | Zones fail independently, so spreading across them survives the most common large failures. | multi-region-deployment         | Multi-region deployment         | 2    | any      | Running the workload in two or more geographically separate regions, with replicated data and global traffic routing.                                                                                         | It survives losing a whole region but adds cost, latency and data-consistency trade-offs: cross-region replication is usually asynchronous, so some recent writes can be lost. | active-active-vs-active-passive | Active-active vs active-passive | 1    | any      | Active-active: every site serves traffic all the time. | failback                        | Failback                        | 2    | any      | Returning traffic to the original primary after it recovers: resynchronising the data written during the failover, then switching back in a controlled way, or simply making the old standby the new primary. | Failback isn't failover in reverse. | automatic-vs-manual-failover    | Automatic vs manual failover    | 3    | any      | Whether software or a person decides to fail over. ### 9. Reliability targets (SRE)

| id                     | Concept             | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Often confused with                            |
| ---------------------- | ------------------- | ---- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| sli-slo-sla            | SLI, SLO and SLA    | 1    | any      | SLI: a measurement of user experience, usually good events ÷ valid events (requests that succeeded, or finished under 300 ms). ### 11. Testing resilience

| id                | Concept                   | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                    | Often confused with                             |
| ----------------- | ------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| chaos-engineering | Chaos engineering         | 2    | any      | Experimenting on a system to build confidence it can withstand turbulent conditions: define steady state as a business metric, hypothesise it will hold, inject a real-world fault (ideally in production, with a limited blast radius) and look for a difference against a control. | You don't really know whether your redundancy, timeouts and failover work until you break something on purpose. ### 12. Disaster recovery

| id                        | Concept                                   | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                 | Often confused with                                   |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| rpo-vs-rto                | RPO vs RTO                                | 1    | any      | RPO (recovery point objective): the most data you can afford to lose, measured as time since the last good copy. RTO (recovery time objective): the longest you can afford to be down.                                                                      | Two clocks on one timeline: RPO looks back from the disaster (how much work disappears), RTO looks forward (how long until you're back). Lowering either costs more.                                   | Each other; MTTR                                      |
| ha-vs-dr                  | High availability vs disaster recovery    | 1    | any      | High availability keeps a service running through common failures (an instance, a zone) automatically, in seconds. Disaster recovery restores it after rare, large events (a lost region, corrupted or deleted data, ransomware) through a planned process. | HA doesn't replace DR: a synchronously replicated database replicates a bad delete in milliseconds. You need both, with different targets.                                                             | Business continuity (the whole business, not just IT) |
| dr-strategies             | DR strategies: from backups to multi-site | 1    | any      | The four standard strategies, cheapest and slowest first: backup and restore (hours), pilot light (tens of minutes), warm standby (minutes), multi-site active-active or hot standby (near zero).                                                           | Choose per workload from the RTO and RPO it needs and what downtime costs. Most workloads don't need active-active; some can't live without it.                                                        | HA; "cold, warm and hot standby" (overlapping names)  |
| dr-backup-and-restore     | Backup and restore (DR strategy)          | 2    | any      | The simplest DR strategy: copy backups of data, plus infrastructure as code, images and configuration, to another region, and rebuild everything from them after a disaster.                                                                                | Cheapest, but recovery takes hours because the whole environment must be built before data can be restored, and it only works if the infrastructure is defined as code.                                | Backup (the data copy alone)                          |
| dr-pilot-light            | Pilot light                               | 2    | any      | A DR strategy where data is continuously replicated to the recovery region and core infrastructure exists, but application servers are switched off or not yet deployed until a disaster.                                                                   | The data is always live, like a gas pilot light; recovery means switching on and scaling up the compute around it. It can't serve a single request until then.                                         | Warm standby                                          |
| dr-warm-standby           | Warm standby                              | 2    | any      | A DR strategy where a scaled-down but fully working copy of production runs in the recovery region, can take traffic immediately, and scales up after failover.                                                                                             | Unlike pilot light it serves requests straight away, so it's easier to test continuously; the remaining risk is depending on scale-up (a control-plane action) during the disaster.                    | Pilot light; hot standby                              |
| replication-is-not-backup | Replication is not backup                 | 1    | any      | Replication copies every change to another place almost instantly; a backup is a separate point-in-time copy you can restore to a moment before the damage.                                                                                                 | Replication protects against losing a machine or a site, not against mistakes: a dropped table or ransomware-encrypted data replicates too. You still need versioned, isolated, point-in-time backups. | Redundancy; durability                                |
