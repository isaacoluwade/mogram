# Fundamentals: what the cloud is, and what it costs

Stage 1 research catalog for the **Fundamentals** area (cloud fundamentals and cost / FinOps).
Researched 2026-10-05.

## Scope

**In:** what cloud computing is and why it exists (the NIST definition, on-premises vs cloud);
service models (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS, CaaS, serverless) and who manages what; deployment models
(public, private, hybrid, multi-cloud, sovereign, distributed/edge) and lock-in; the shared
responsibility model; global infrastructure (regions, zones, edge locations, resource scope,
choosing a region, residency and sovereignty); compute options compared; resource organisation
and governance (hierarchies, accounts as boundaries, tags, policies, landing zones, quotas); how
the cloud is driven (portal, CLI, SDK, API) and the control plane vs data plane; provider-level
multi-tenancy; managed-service trade-offs; cloud economics (CapEx/OpEx, pay-as-you-go, economies
of scale, TCO, cost drivers, licensing); pricing models (on-demand, commitments, spot, data
transfer); FinOps (lifecycle, allocation, showback/chargeback, unit economics, budgets, cost
data) and the cost-optimisation levers (rate vs usage, rightsizing, idle, scheduling,
autoscaling for cost, storage tiering); well-architected pillars as a whole; cloud adoption
frameworks and migration strategies (the 7 Rs); data gravity; provider SLAs and composite SLAs;
sustainability.

**Left to other areas** (listed under Overlaps): block/file/object storage and storage mechanics
(Data); high availability, redundancy, failover, DR, SLI/SLO/error budgets (Reliability);
vertical/horizontal scaling and autoscaling mechanics (Scaling); identity, IAM, RBAC, least
privilege, encryption, compliance frameworks (Security); virtual networks, VPN/interconnect,
DNS, CDN (Networking); containers and Kubernetes themselves (Containers); infrastructure as code
(Delivery); serverless and event-driven application architecture, modernisation patterns
(Architecture); monitoring and service health (Observability); GPU capacity and AI cost
(AI systems). Every platform's own names and products (management groups, AWS Organizations and
SCPs, GCP folders, Savings Plans, Azure Hybrid Benefit, etc.) belong in `azure.md`, `aws.md`
and `gcp.md`.

All concepts here are platform-neutral (`any`): each exists, under different names, on all three
major clouds.

### 8. Cloud economics

| id                       | Concept                                           | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Often confused with                             |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| capex-vs-opex            | CapEx vs OpEx                                     | 1    | any      | Capital expenditure is paying upfront for assets you own and depreciate (servers, data centres); operational expenditure is paying as you go for services you consume.                                                          | The cloud turns a big, fixed, upfront bet into a variable cost that tracks usage: less risk of buying the wrong thing, faster to start and stop. But the bill is now variable, and someone has to manage it.                                               | "Cheaper" (OpEx isn't automatically less money) |
| pay-as-you-go            | Pay-as-you-go (consumption-based) pricing         | 1    | any      | Billing by metered use (per second, hour, GB-month, request or execution) with no upfront commitment: NIST's "measured service".                                                                                                | "Pay for what you use" really means "pay for what you leave running": a provisioned VM bills while idle, while per-request services bill only when used. Metering is what turns cost into an engineering concern.                                          | Free (no upfront cost isn't low cost)           |
| economies-of-scale       | Economies of scale                                | 2    | any      | Providers buy hardware, power and network capacity in enormous volume, build custom hardware and keep utilisation high across millions of customers, which lowers their cost per unit.                                          | Aggregated demand lets a provider run each unit cheaper than any single customer could, and part of that saving reaches customers as lower prices and price cuts over time. It's the main reason renting can beat owning.                                  | Volume discounts on your own usage              |
| total-cost-of-ownership  | Total cost of ownership (TCO)                     | 2    | any      | The full cost of running a workload over its life. On-premises: hardware, space, power, cooling, network, licences, staff and refresh cycles. Cloud: the services plus data transfer, support, migration and skills.            | Comparing a VM's hourly price to a server's purchase price misleads; compare everything. The cloud usually wins for variable or uncertain demand, while large, steady, predictable workloads can be cheaper owned, which is why some companies repatriate. | A cloud bill compared with a hardware quote     |
| cloud-cost-drivers       | What drives a cloud bill                          | 1    | any      | The meters behind a cloud bill: compute time, storage capacity, operations and requests, data transfer, IP addresses and gateways, log ingestion and retention, licences, support plans, premium tiers, and the region's price. | Compute is rarely the whole story. The surprises are the hidden costs: small meters (NAT gateways, cross-zone traffic, log ingestion, API requests, idle IPs, old snapshots) that grow silently with architecture choices.                                 | A VM's hourly sticker price                     |
| byol-vs-license-included | Licence-included vs bring your own licence (BYOL) | 3    | any      | Paying for commercial software licences (operating systems, databases) inside the hourly price, or applying licences you already own to cloud resources.                                                                        | Licences can cost more than the infrastructure, and many are counted per core, so the licence model drives instance choice, dedicated hosts and migration plans. Licences you already own can make the cloud cheaper than list price suggests.             | `commitment-discounts`                          |

| spot-instances          | Spot and preemptible capacity                                     | 2    | any      | The provider's spare capacity, sold at a steep discount (often 60 to 90 percent off), which it can reclaim at short notice (seconds to a couple of minutes) when it needs it back.                                                                                                         | Spot is cheap because it can disappear. It suits stateless, fault-tolerant, checkpointed or batch work (CI, rendering, data processing, extra scale-out nodes) and is dangerous for anything that can't be interrupted.                                                                                                                    | `commitment-discounts` (a discount for staying, not for being evictable) |
| data-transfer-costs     | Data transfer (egress) costs                                      | 1    | any      | Charges for moving data: into the cloud is usually free; out to the internet is charged per GB; between regions, and often between zones, is charged too; NAT gateways and load balancers add processing charges.                                                                          | Data is cheap to bring in and expensive to take out, so architecture shapes the bill: chatty cross-zone or cross-region designs, serving downloads from origin instead of a CDN, and data flowing between clouds all pay a toll per GB.                                                                                                    | Bandwidth limits (a performance ceiling, not a price)                    |

### 10. FinOps practice

| id                              | Concept                                     | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Often confused with                                              |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| finops                          | FinOps                                      | 1    | any      | An operating framework and culture in which engineering, finance, product, procurement and leadership work together on the variable cost of cloud (and, increasingly, SaaS, AI and data centres) so that spending drives business value.       | FinOps isn't about spending less; it's about spending well. Engineers own the cost of what they build, decisions use timely cost data, and a central team enables rather than polices. Maturity grows crawl, walk, run.                   | Cost cutting; cloud accounting or procurement                    |
| finops-lifecycle                | FinOps lifecycle: inform, optimise, operate | 2    | any      | The three iterative phases: Inform (visibility, allocation, budgets, forecasts), Optimise (find efficiency through usage and rate changes), Operate (put the changes into practice, govern, and keep improving).                               | It's a loop run again and again, not a project: each turn of visibility, opportunity and action improves the next, and different teams can be in different phases at once.                                                                | A one-off cost-reduction exercise                                |
| cost-allocation                 | Cost allocation                             | 2    | any      | Assigning every cost to the team, product, environment or cost centre that caused it, using account structure, tags and agreed rules for splitting shared costs (proportional, even or fixed).                                                 | You can't optimise what nobody owns. Allocation turns one big bill into many owned bills; shared costs (networking, support, platform teams) need a split rule, or they become everybody's and nobody's.                                  | `showback-vs-chargeback` (what you do with costs once allocated) |
| showback-vs-chargeback          | Showback vs chargeback                      | 2    | any      | Showback reports each team's allocated costs back to them; chargeback formally bills those costs to their budgets in the finance system.                                                                                                       | Both show cost to the people who cause it; only chargeback moves money. Showback is always needed; chargeback is an optional, heavier step that only works once allocation is trusted.                                                    | `cost-allocation`                                                |
| unit-economics                  | Unit economics                              | 2    | any      | Expressing cloud cost per unit of business value or work: cost per customer, per order, per transaction, per GB stored, per AI token.                                                                                                          | A rising bill can be good news: if cost per customer falls while the bill grows with revenue, you're scaling efficiently. Total spend alone can't tell growth from waste; cost per unit can.                                              | Reducing total spend                                             |
| cost-estimation-and-forecasting | Cost estimation and forecasting             | 2    | any      | Estimating what a design will cost before it's built (pricing calculators, cost models) and forecasting future spend from trends, growth and planned changes.                                                                                  | Cost is a design input, not a surprise afterwards: estimate while the architecture is still cheap to change, then forecast continuously so budgets and commitments rest on expected usage rather than last month's bill.                  | Budgets (a limit you set, not a prediction)                      |
| budgets-and-anomaly-alerts      | Budgets and cost anomaly alerts             | 1    | any      | Budgets set expected spend for an account, project or tag and alert at thresholds of actual or forecast spend; anomaly detection flags spend that departs from the usual pattern, such as a runaway job or a leaked key mining cryptocurrency. | A budget is an alarm, not a brake: nothing stops spending when it's exceeded unless you wire up automation. Anomaly alerts catch a sudden spike within hours instead of on the month-end invoice.                                         | Spending caps; `quotas-and-limits`                               |
| cost-and-usage-data             | Cost and usage data                         | 3    | any      | The detailed billing export (line items per resource per hour, with list, billed and effective or amortised cost, and tags) that feeds every FinOps report. The line-item data, with commitment purchases spread over the usage they cover and tags attached, is the source of truth for allocation, showback and optimisation; comparing clouds needs one schema. | The invoice; a cost dashboard                                    |

### 11. Cost optimisation levers

| id                         | Concept                                 | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                               | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Often confused with                                                         |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| rate-vs-usage-optimization | Rate optimisation vs usage optimisation | 2    | any      | The two levers on any cloud cost (rate times quantity): pay less per unit (commitments, spot, negotiated discounts, licensing) or use fewer units (rightsizing, switching off, scaling in, cheaper architectures).       | Usage is usually owned by engineers and rate by a central FinOps team. Cut usage first, then commit to the smaller baseline, or you lock discounts onto waste.                                                                           | Cost cutting in general                                                     |
| rightsizing                | Rightsizing                             | 1    | any      | Matching a resource's size and type to its measured utilisation: moving a VM that averages 10 percent CPU to a smaller size, a better-fitting family or a newer, cheaper generation, and consolidating sparse workloads. | Most resources are sized for a guess, not for reality. Looking at weeks of real CPU, memory and I/O and stepping down one size roughly halves that line. It's continuous, because workloads change.                                      | Autoscaling (changes how many, not how big)                                 |
| cost-of-idle               | Cost of idle and orphaned resources     | 1    | any      | Spend on capacity that's allocated but doing nothing: idle VMs, over-provisioned clusters, unattached disks, old snapshots, unused IP addresses and load balancers, forgotten test environments.                         | You pay for what's allocated, not what's used, and nothing reminds you it's there. Idle is the largest and easiest source of waste to remove once tags and ownership show who can delete it.                                             | `rightsizing` (shrinks something in use; this removes something unused)     |
| schedule-non-prod-off      | Scheduling non-production off           | 2    | any      | Automatically stopping development, test and other non-production resources outside working hours and starting them again when needed.                                                                                   | A week has 168 hours; a working week about 50. Switching non-production off at nights and weekends cuts its compute bill by around two-thirds with no change to the architecture.                                                        | Deleting environments; autoscaling                                          |
| autoscaling-for-cost       | Autoscaling for cost                    | 2    | any      | Using automatic scaling so capacity, and therefore spend, follows demand, with sensible minimums, deliberate scale-in rules and scheduled scaling for known patterns.                                                    | Provisioning for peak means paying for peak around the clock; the gap between the peak line and the demand curve is waste. The savings come from scaling in, so scale-in deserves as much care as scale-out.                             | `elasticity-vs-scalability` (the property); autoscaling mechanics (Scaling) |
| storage-tiering-for-cost   | Storage tiering for cost                | 2    | any      | Placing data in storage tiers by how often it's read (hot, cool, cold, archive), usually with lifecycle rules that move or delete it automatically as it ages.                                                           | Cold tiers are cheap to keep and expensive to read (retrieval fees, minimum storage periods, slow retrieval), so the right tier depends on how often data is read, not how much there is. Deleting data you don't need beats every tier. | Backup and archive (Data)                                                   |

### 12. Frameworks, adoption and provider promises

| well-architected-pillars | Well-Architected pillars                  | 2    | any      | The providers' frameworks for reviewing a workload's design, built on shared pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency and cost optimisation, plus sustainability (AWS and Google; Azure has five pillars).                                                           | The pillars pull against each other (more redundancy is more reliable and more expensive), so a well-architected design is a set of explicit, reviewed trade-offs for this workload's needs, not the maximum of everything.                       | Compliance frameworks; `cloud-adoption-framework`                                                   |

| composite-sla            | Composite SLA                             | 2    | any      | The combined availability of a workload built from several services: for components in series, multiply their availabilities; for redundant copies in parallel, multiply their chances of failure.                                                                                                         | Every dependency in the chain lowers the total (three 99.9 percent services in series give about 99.7 percent), while redundancy raises it (two independent 99 percent copies give 99.99 percent). | cloud-sustainability     | Cloud sustainability and carbon awareness | 3    | any      | The environmental footprint of cloud workloads and how to cut it: the provider handles sustainability of the cloud (efficient facilities, renewable power); the customer handles sustainability in the cloud (utilisation, rightsizing, data kept, and region and timing chosen by grid carbon intensity). | The greenest resource is the one you don't run, so most cost optimisation is also carbon optimisation; flexible work can also shift to cleaner regions or hours (carbon-aware computing).                                                         | Cost optimisation (closely aligned but not identical: a cheap region can have a dirty grid)         |
