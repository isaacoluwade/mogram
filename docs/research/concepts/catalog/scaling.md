# Scaling & performance: concept catalog

89 concepts in 10 groups: 18 tier 1, 59 tier 2, 12 tier 3. Seven of them are already shipped
(marked **shipped**): `horizontal-scaling`, `vertical-scaling`, `scale-up-vs-out`, `autoscaling`,
`load-balancing`, `caching`, `queue-load-levelling`. The other two shipped concepts this area touches,
`failover` and `canary`, belong to Reliability and Delivery and are listed under Overlaps.

## Scope

**In:** how a system gets more capacity, and how it uses the capacity it has.

- Scaling: vertical and horizontal, statelessness, session affinity, scale units, elasticity.
- Autoscaling: reactive (target tracking), scheduled and predictive scaling, metrics, lag, cooldowns,
  flapping, scale to zero, cold starts and warm pools.
- Load balancing as a scaling tool: algorithms, hashing, health checks, draining, global vs regional.
- Caching: why it works, where caches live, read and write strategies, freshness, eviction, stampedes.
- Content delivery and edge compute.
- Performance fundamentals: latency, throughput, percentiles, tail latency, Little's Law, queueing,
  bottlenecks, the critical path, Amdahl's law, concurrency.
- Doing less work per request: async I/O, pooling, batching, compression, pagination, N+1,
  precomputation, and how reads and writes scale differently.
- Protecting capacity: overload, rate limiting and its algorithms, throttling, quotas, load shedding,
  backpressure, queue load levelling, admission control, noisy neighbours.
- Capacity planning, headroom, load/stress/soak/spike testing, right-sizing.

**Left to other areas** (see Overlaps): L4 vs L7, reverse proxies, anycast and DNS mechanics
(Networking); sharding, partitioning, read replicas, indexing, denormalisation, hot partitions
(Data); retries, backoff, circuit breakers, bulkheads, cascading failure, graceful degradation,
failover (Reliability); USE/RED/golden signals, profiling, SLOs (Observability); microservices,
serverless, CQRS, deployment stamps, gateways (Architecture); queues, competing consumers, priority
queues (Messaging); spot capacity, pricing models, instance families (Fundamentals); HPA, VPA,
cluster autoscaler, KEDA (Containers); GPU capacity and token rate limits (AI systems); every named
cloud product (Azure, AWS, Google Cloud).

**Boundary decisions to reconcile in `CATALOG.md`:**

1. `load-balancing` and `queue-load-levelling` are catalogued **here**. The README puts load balancing
   and capacity protection in Scaling. The shipped cards still say `topic: networking` and
   `topic: messaging`, so Networking and Messaging should list them as overlaps, and the cards'
   `topic` may need changing (this file doesn't touch them).
2. `consistent-hashing` is owned here because it's a load-balancing and cache-placement tool first.
   Data should list it as an overlap from sharding.
3. `n-plus-one-queries` is owned here as a performance antipattern (Azure's Chatty I/O). Data lists it
   as an overlap. `read-replicas`, `sharding` and `denormalisation` belong to Data. This file only
   points at them, through `scaling-reads-vs-writes`.
4. `materialised-view` is owned here, as precomputation for reads. Architecture should list it as an
   overlap from CQRS, and Data as an overlap from denormalisation.
5. `health-checks` here are load-balancer health checks. Reliability owns the Health Endpoint
   Monitoring pattern and Containers owns liveness/readiness probes, with each side listing the other.
6. `cdn`, `edge-compute` and `static-content-hosting` are owned here, as the brief assigns content
   delivery to Scaling. Networking and Architecture list them as overlaps.
7. `backpressure` is owned here. Messaging lists it as an overlap, because stream consumers use it.
8. `right-sizing` is owned here. Spot/preemptible capacity and pricing models belong to Fundamentals.
9. Spelling follows the shipped ids (British: `levelling`, so `utilisation`, `materialised`,
   `neighbour`).

### 1. Capacity and scaling basics

| id                      | Concept                             | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                            | Often confused with                                                                         |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `scalability`           | Scalability (vs performance)        | 1    | any      | The ability to handle more load by adding resources, with throughput growing in step with what's added.                                                                   | A performance problem makes the system slow for one user. A scalability problem means it's fast for one user and slow under load. In a scalable system, each resource added buys about the same extra throughput. | Performance; elasticity                                                                     |
| `elasticity`            | Elasticity                          | 1    | any      | Capacity that grows and shrinks automatically to follow demand, hour by hour.                                                                                             | Fixed capacity is always wrong. Sized for the peak, it sits idle and paid for most of the day. Sized for the average, it drops requests at the peak. Elastic capacity follows the demand curve.                   | Scalability (the ability to grow at all; elasticity is growing and shrinking automatically) |
| `vertical-scaling`      | Vertical scaling (**shipped**)      | 1    | any      | Give one machine more CPU, memory or disk as its load grows.                                                                                                              | It's simple and needs no code changes, but there's always a largest size. Resizing usually means a restart, and it's still one machine that can fail.                                                             | Horizontal scaling                                                                          |
| `horizontal-scaling`    | Horizontal scaling (**shipped**)    | 1    | any      | Run more copies of a service behind a load balancer as traffic grows, and fewer as it falls.                                                                              | Capacity grows by adding copies, with no practical ceiling, and losing one copy loses only its share. It only works if any copy can serve any request.                                                            | Vertical scaling; sharding (scaling data out, not compute)                                  |
| `scale-up-vs-out`       | Scale up vs scale out (**shipped**) | 1    | any      | A side-by-side comparison of making one machine bigger and adding more machines.                                                                                          | Scaling up is quick and simple, but it hits a ceiling and keeps a single point of failure. Scaling out keeps growing and survives failures, but it needs stateless services and a load balancer.                  |                                                                                             |
| `stateless-vs-stateful` | Stateless vs stateful services      | 1    | any      | A stateless service keeps nothing between requests in its own memory: sessions, carts and uploads live in a shared store such as a cache or database.                     | This is why stateless scales: if any copy can serve any request, copies can be added, removed or replaced freely. Moving session state out to a shared store turns a stateful service into a stateless one.       | "Stateless" meaning the system has no state (the state has moved somewhere shared)          |
| `sticky-sessions`       | Sticky sessions (session affinity)  | 2    | any      | The load balancer pins each user to the same instance, usually with a cookie, so that user's in-memory session is there on every request.                                 | Stickiness makes a stateful app work behind a load balancer, at a price. Load spreads unevenly, new instances only get new users, and when an instance dies or scales in, its users lose their sessions.          | IP hash; consistent hashing                                                                 |
| `scale-unit`            | Scale units                         | 3    | any      | A fixed bundle of resources that scale together in proportion, e.g. 10 app instances, one cache and one queue partition. A whole copy of the stack is a deployment stamp. | Scaling one tier on its own just moves the bottleneck to the next tier. Scaling in tested units keeps every part in proportion and makes capacity predictable ("one unit serves 50,000 users").                   | Autoscaling one resource; deployment stamps (the Architecture pattern built on it)          |

### 2. Autoscaling

| id                     | Concept                             | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                         | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Often confused with                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `autoscaling`          | Autoscaling (**shipped**)           | 1    | any      | The platform adds and removes instances automatically, based on a metric and within a minimum and a maximum.                                                       | Set the fewest copies (for safety), the most (for cost) and a target load. Copies are added above the target and removed below it, but never past those limits.                                                                                                              | Elasticity (the property; autoscaling is the mechanism)                  |
| `target-tracking`      | Target tracking scaling             | 2    | any      | A scaling policy that holds a metric at a set value, like a thermostat: "keep average CPU at 50%".                                                                 | It scales in proportion. At 80% CPU against a 50% target, it asks for 1.6 times the instances in one step, instead of adding one at a time the way a threshold (step) rule does.                                                                                             | Step (threshold) scaling                                                 |
| `scaling-metrics`      | Choosing a scaling metric           | 2    | any      | The signal autoscaling watches: CPU, requests per instance, queue backlog per worker, latency, or a custom metric.                                                 | A good metric rises with load and falls as copies are added. CPU suits CPU-bound web servers and backlog per worker suits queue workers: a video transcoder at 100% CPU is doing its job, not overloaded. Raw requests per second misleads, because requests differ in cost. | Monitoring metrics in general; requests per second as a capacity measure |
| `scheduled-scaling`    | Scheduled scaling                   | 2    | any      | Change capacity at set times for load you know is coming, such as the weekday 9 a.m. rush, month-end or a launch.                                                  | When you know a spike is coming, don't wait for metrics to notice it. Have the capacity ready first, and let reactive scaling handle the surprises.                                                                                                                          | Predictive scaling                                                       |
| `predictive-scaling`   | Predictive scaling                  | 2    | any      | The platform learns daily and weekly patterns from history, forecasts the next hours, and scales ahead of them.                                                    | Reactive scaling is always late by at least the boot time. Forecasting a repeating curve launches capacity before the rise, so the morning ramp doesn't start on an undersized fleet.                                                                                        | Scheduled scaling (you set the times; predictive learns them)            |
| `autoscaling-lag`      | Autoscaling lag                     | 2    | any      | The delay between load rising and new capacity serving it: collecting the metric, evaluating the alarm, provisioning, booting and warming up.                      | Autoscaling takes minutes. A spike that arrives in seconds lands on the fleet you already have, so headroom, warm pools, queues or load shedding have to cover the gap.                                                                                                      | Cooldown                                                                 |
| `autoscaling-cooldown` | Cooldowns and stabilisation windows | 2    | any      | A pause after a scaling action, or a look-back window before scaling in, during which the scaler won't act again.                                                  | New instances take time to boot and take load off the metric. Without a pause, the scaler sees the old high reading and keeps adding. Scale out fast; scale in slowly.                                                                                                       | Flapping (the problem); autoscaling lag                                  |
| `autoscaling-flapping` | Flapping                            | 2    | any      | Scaling that oscillates. A scale-out drops the metric below the scale-in threshold, and the scale-in pushes it back over the scale-out threshold, again and again. | Leave a wide gap between the two thresholds (e.g. out above 70%, in below 40%), so that the reading after any scaling step lands in the quiet zone between them.                                                                                                             | Cooldown; normal scaling under changing load                             |
| `scale-to-zero`        | Scale to zero                       | 2    | any      | When there's no traffic, run no instances at all, and start one on the next request.                                                                               | Idle time costs nothing, which suits spiky or rarely used services. The trade is that the first request after a quiet spell pays a cold start. A minimum of one instance costs a little and removes it.                                                                      | Serverless (scale to zero is one property of it)                         |
| `cold-start`           | Cold start                          | 2    | any      | The extra latency of a request that has to wait for a new instance or function to start: provisioning, booting, loading code, opening connections, warming up.     | Scaling out and scaling from zero aren't free, because the first requests to a new instance are slow. Smaller packages, lazy initialisation, minimum instances and warm pools shrink the delay. Load balancers' slow-start mode ramps traffic to new instances gradually.    | Cold cache (an empty cache on an instance that's already running)        |
| `warm-pool`            | Warm pools and pre-warmed capacity  | 2    | any      | Instances or function environments initialised ahead of time and held stopped or idle, ready to join quickly (e.g. provisioned concurrency, minimum instances).    | Pay a little to keep capacity half-started, and a scale-out takes seconds instead of minutes. The cost is the idle pool.                                                                                                                                                     | Headroom (spare capacity that's already serving)                         |

### 3. Load balancing

| id                      | Concept                              | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                              | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Often confused with                                                        |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `load-balancing`        | Load balancing (**shipped**)         | 1    | any      | A load balancer accepts requests on one address and spreads them across the servers behind it.                                                                          | Clients see one service. Behind it, servers can be added, removed or fail without clients noticing, and each carries a fair share.                                                                                                                                                | Reverse proxy; DNS round robin                                             |
| `round-robin`           | Round robin and weighted round robin | 2    | any      | Send requests to the servers in turn. Weighted round robin gives bigger servers proportionally more turns.                                                              | It's simple and fair when every request costs the same. It doesn't look at how busy a server is, though: a slow server, or a run of heavy requests, piles work onto one server that keeps getting its turn.                                                                       | Least connections                                                          |
| `least-connections`     | Least connections                    | 2    | any      | Send each new request to the server with the fewest requests in progress.                                                                                               | Busy servers have more open requests, so this steers work away from them, which beats round robin when request lengths vary. Picking the less busy of two random servers ("power of two choices") gets most of the benefit, and stops many balancers piling onto the same server. | Round robin; least response time                                           |
| `least-response-time`   | Least response time                  | 3    | any      | Send each request to the server that's answering fastest, often combined with the fewest active requests.                                                               | It reacts to slowness, not just to queue length. But a broken server that fails instantly looks like the fastest one and draws all the traffic, unless errors count against it.                                                                                                   | Least connections                                                          |
| `ip-hash`               | IP hash (hash-based routing)         | 2    | any      | Hash the client's IP (or a header or cookie) to choose the server, so the same client always lands on the same server.                                                  | It gives affinity without the balancer storing anything. But everyone behind one NAT lands on the same server, and adding or removing a server reshuffles almost every client (the modulo problem).                                                                               | Sticky sessions (cookie-based); consistent hashing                         |
| `consistent-hashing`    | Consistent hashing                   | 2    | any      | Servers and keys are placed around a hash ring, and each key belongs to the next server clockwise. Virtual nodes even out the spread.                                   | When a server joins or leaves, only the keys next to it move (about 1/n of them) instead of nearly all of them. Caches stay warm and affinity survives scaling.                                                                                                                   | Plain hash-mod-N; sharding (consistent hashing is one way to place shards) |
| `health-checks`         | Health checks                        | 1    | any      | The load balancer probes each server regularly (e.g. GET /health) and stops sending it traffic after several failures in a row, until it passes again.                  | Failed servers are routed around in seconds, with nobody acting. A shallow check proves the process is up. A deep check that also tests a shared database can fail every server at once and take the whole service down.                                                          | Kubernetes liveness and readiness probes; monitoring                       |
| `connection-draining`   | Connection draining                  | 2    | any      | When a server is being removed (scale-in, deployment), the load balancer stops sending it new requests but lets the requests already in flight finish, up to a timeout. | Taking a server out shouldn't cut off the users it's serving. Stop new work, let the old work finish, then shut down.                                                                                                                                                             | Health-check removal (abrupt, after failure; draining is planned)          |
| `global-load-balancing` | Global vs regional load balancing    | 2    | any      | A global load balancer (DNS-based or anycast) sends each user to the best region. A regional one spreads traffic across servers and zones inside a region.              | There are two layers: the global one picks the nearest healthy region, and the regional one picks the server. When a region fails, the global layer sends its users to the next nearest region.                                                                                   | CDN; DNS failover; anycast (one way to build it)                           |

### 4. Caching: why it works and where it lives

| id                      | Concept                                  | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                    | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Often confused with                                                                      |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `caching`               | Caching (**shipped**)                    | 1    | any      | A cache keeps copies of answers it has already fetched, so repeat requests are served without going back to the source.                                                       | Hits come straight back and only misses go on to the database, so the database carries a fraction of the traffic and responses are faster.                                                                                     | Read replicas (full copies kept in sync; a cache holds a partial, possibly stale subset) |
| `locality-of-reference` | Why caches work: locality and popularity | 2    | any      | Requests aren't spread evenly. What was asked for recently is likely to be asked for again, nearby data is often next, and a small set of items gets most of the traffic.     | Because popularity is so skewed, a cache that holds a small fraction of the data can serve most of the requests. If every request were for something different, no cache could help.                                           | Prefetching                                                                              |
| `cache-hit-ratio`       | Cache hit ratio                          | 2    | any      | The share of requests served from the cache instead of the source.                                                                                                            | The source only sees the misses, so going from 90% to 99% hits cuts its load tenfold (from 10% of traffic to 1%). Small changes in hit ratio make big changes in backend load.                                                 | Cache size (a bigger cache only helps up to the size of the popular set)                 |
| `cache-layers`          | Where caches live                        | 1    | any      | Caches sit at every hop: the browser, the CDN edge, a reverse proxy, the application (in-process or shared) and the database's own memory.                                    | Each layer answers what it can and passes the rest on. The closer to the user a hit happens, the more round trips and work it saves, but every layer is one more copy that can be stale.                                       |                                                                                          |
| `browser-cache`         | Browser cache and HTTP caching           | 1    | any      | The browser keeps responses for as long as the Cache-Control header allows. Once one expires, it asks again with an ETag and gets a tiny 304 Not Modified if nothing changed. | The fastest request is the one never sent. The server decides how long clients may reuse a response, and revalidation turns a full download into a quick "still good?" check.                                                  | CDN cache (shared at the edge; the browser cache is private to one user)                 |
| `reverse-proxy-cache`   | Reverse proxy cache                      | 2    | any      | A proxy in front of the app servers (e.g. NGINX, Varnish) stores whole responses and serves repeats itself.                                                                   | A popular page is built once and served many times. App servers only see the misses and the personalised requests.                                                                                                             | CDN (the same idea spread around the world); load balancer                               |
| `distributed-cache`     | Distributed cache (vs in-process cache)  | 2    | any      | A shared cache cluster (e.g. Redis, Memcached) that every app instance reads and writes, instead of each instance keeping its own cache in memory.                            | In-process caches each warm up separately and disagree after an update. A shared cache holds one copy that every instance sees, at the cost of a network hop and one more service to run. Big caches are sharded across nodes. | A database (a cache is fast and disposable, not the source of truth)                     |
| `cold-cache`            | Cold cache and cache warming             | 2    | any      | An empty cache after a restart, deployment, failover or flush. Warming fills it before traffic arrives, or while traffic ramps up.                                            | A backend sized for the cache's misses can't survive 100% misses. When the cache is empty or down, all traffic hits the source at once. Warm caches before cutting over, and ramp traffic slowly.                              | Cold start (a new instance booting)                                                      |

### 5. Caching: strategies, freshness and failure modes

| id                       | Concept                                  | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                          | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Often confused with                                                            |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `cache-aside`            | Cache-aside (lazy loading)               | 2    | any      | The application checks the cache first. On a miss it reads from the database and puts the result into the cache itself.                                                                                             | Only data that's actually requested gets cached, and a cache outage just means slower reads. The costs: every first read is a miss, and the application has to invalidate entries when it writes.                                                      | Read-through                                                                   |
| `read-through`           | Read-through cache                       | 2    | any      | The application only talks to the cache. On a miss, the cache loads from the database itself.                                                                                                                       | It fills lazily like cache-aside, but the loading logic lives in the cache layer, so application code stays simple and every caller loads data the same way.                                                                                           | Cache-aside                                                                    |
| `write-through`          | Write-through cache                      | 2    | any      | Every write goes to the cache and the database together, before the write is acknowledged.                                                                                                                          | The cache never serves stale data for anything written through it. The price is slower writes, and caching data that may never be read.                                                                                                                | Write-behind                                                                   |
| `write-behind`           | Write-behind (write-back) cache          | 3    | any      | Writes go to the cache and are acknowledged at once. The cache saves them to the database later, often in batches.                                                                                                  | Writes are very fast and the database sees fewer of them. But if the cache dies before it saves them, those writes are lost: durability has been traded for speed.                                                                                     | Write-through; a message queue                                                 |
| `cache-ttl`              | Time to live (TTL)                       | 1    | any      | Each cached entry expires after a set time, and the next request fetches it fresh.                                                                                                                                  | TTL is a dial between freshness and load. A short TTL means less stale data but more misses; a long one means more hits but older data. It limits how stale anything can get without tracking changes.                                                 | Cache invalidation (removal triggered by a change)                             |
| `cache-invalidation`     | Cache invalidation                       | 2    | any      | Removing or updating cached copies when the source changes: delete on write, purge a CDN path, publish a change event.                                                                                              | Data changes while copies of it live on. Invalidating on change keeps caches fresh without short TTLs. Races (a slow read refilling the cache with old data just after the delete) make it notoriously hard, so most systems keep a TTL as a backstop. | TTL; eviction                                                                  |
| `cache-eviction`         | Eviction policies (LRU, LFU)             | 2    | any      | When the cache is full, it chooses what to drop: least recently used (LRU), least frequently used (LFU), oldest first, or random.                                                                                   | Eviction is about space, not freshness. LRU keeps whatever is hot now, but one big scan can flush it. LFU keeps long-time favourites, but is slow to let go of yesterday's.                                                                            | Expiry (TTL)                                                                   |
| `stale-while-revalidate` | Stale-while-revalidate and refresh-ahead | 3    | any      | Serve an expired entry for a short grace period while a fresh one is fetched in the background. Refresh-ahead does the refresh just before expiry, and stale-if-error serves the old copy while the source is down. | Users never wait for a refresh, and a source outage can hide behind slightly old data. A soft expiry keeps data fresh; a hard expiry keeps it correct.                                                                                                 | TTL; stampede fixes                                                            |
| `cache-stampede`         | Cache stampede (thundering herd)         | 2    | any      | A popular entry expires, or the cache restarts, and many requests miss at the same moment, all going to the database for the same thing.                                                                            | The cache was hiding how much load there really was: one expiry turns 10,000 hits into 10,000 identical database queries at once. Request coalescing, locks, jittered TTLs and early refresh prevent it.                                               | Retry storm; cold cache                                                        |
| `request-coalescing`     | Request coalescing (single flight)       | 3    | any      | When many requests miss on the same key at once, only one goes to the source, and the rest wait for and share its answer.                                                                                           | 1,000 identical concurrent misses become one backend call. This is the standard fix for stampedes, in CDNs ("collapsed forwarding") and in application caches.                                                                                         | Batching (which combines different requests; coalescing merges identical ones) |
| `negative-caching`       | Negative caching                         | 3    | any      | Caching "not found" and error answers for a short time.                                                                                                                                                             | By default, lookups for things that don't exist always miss and go all the way to the database. Caching the absence briefly stops repeated or malicious lookups hammering the source.                                                                  | Caching errors for a long time (negative entries need short TTLs)              |

### 6. Content delivery and the edge

### 7. Latency, throughput and the maths of performance

| id                           | Concept                                | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                          | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Often confused with                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `latency-vs-throughput`      | Latency, throughput and bandwidth      | 1    | any      | Latency is how long one request takes. Throughput is how many requests complete per second. Bandwidth is the most a link could carry.                               | Think of a motorway: bandwidth is the number of lanes, latency is the journey time, and throughput is the cars arriving per hour. More lanes don't make any car faster. The goal is the most throughput at acceptable latency.         | Bandwidth and speed                                                         |
| `latency-percentiles`        | Latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99)    | 1    | any      | Percentiles rank all request times: p50 is the median, and p99 is slower than 99% of requests.                                                                      | Averages hide the slow requests that users actually feel: a 100 ms average can hide a 2-second p99. The heaviest users make the most requests, so they hit the slow tail most often.                                                   | Average latency                                                             |
| `tail-latency`               | Tail latency at fan-out                | 2    | any      | When one request fans out to many backends, it waits for the slowest of them.                                                                                       | Rare slowness becomes common. If each backend is slow 1% of the time, a request that touches 100 of them is slow 63% of the time. At scale, the tail is what users experience.                                                         | Percentiles (how slowness is measured, not how fan-out multiplies it)       |
| `hedged-requests`            | Hedged requests                        | 3    | any      | If a request hasn't been answered within, say, the p95 time, send a copy to another replica and use whichever answer comes first.                                   | A few percent of extra load can cut the tail dramatically, because the second copy rarely hits the same slow moment.                                                                                                                   | Retries (which wait for a failure; hedging races against slowness)          |
| `latency-numbers`            | Latency numbers to know                | 2    | any      | Rough orders of magnitude: memory read ~100 ns, SSD read ~0.1 ms, round trip inside a data centre ~0.5 ms, disk seek ~10 ms, round trip across an ocean ~150 ms.    | Each step up is ten to a thousand times the last. One round trip across an ocean takes as long as over a million memory reads, so cutting round trips and moving data closer beats almost any code tuning.                             |                                                                             |
| `littles-law`                | Little's Law                           | 2    | any      | Requests in the system = arrival rate × time each one spends there (L = λW).                                                                                        | It links three numbers you can measure. 100 requests a second at 0.2 s each means 20 in flight at once, which is how many threads, connections or instances you need. If latency doubles at the same rate, so does concurrency.        |                                                                             |
| `utilisation-vs-latency`     | Utilisation vs waiting time (queueing) | 2    | any      | As a resource gets busier, requests queue for it, and the wait grows faster and faster as it nears 100% busy.                                                       | The wait isn't linear. With random arrivals, a request at 50% busy takes about twice its work time, and at 90% about ten times. That's why healthy systems run well below 100%, and why any queueing (saturation) is the warning sign. | Efficiency (100% utilisation sounds efficient; for latency it's a disaster) |
| `bottleneck`                 | Bottlenecks                            | 1    | any      | The one resource or stage that limits the whole system's throughput.                                                                                                | A pipeline only goes as fast as its slowest stage, so adding capacity anywhere else changes nothing. Fix the bottleneck and the next-slowest stage takes its place.                                                                    | Critical path (about latency, not throughput)                               |
| `critical-path`              | Critical path                          | 2    | any      | The longest chain of steps that must happen one after another to serve a request.                                                                                   | The critical path sets end-to-end latency. Speeding up a step that isn't on it changes nothing, and running independent calls in parallel instead of in sequence shortens it.                                                          | Bottleneck                                                                  |
| `amdahls-law`                | Amdahl's law                           | 3    | any      | The speed-up you get from parallelising is limited by the part of the work that must run in sequence.                                                               | If 5% of the work can't be split, no number of machines makes it more than 20 times faster. Add contention and coordination (the Universal Scalability Law), and extra machines can even slow things down.                             | Linear scaling                                                              |
| `concurrency-vs-parallelism` | Concurrency vs parallelism             | 2    | any      | Concurrency is having many tasks in progress at once and switching between them. Parallelism is running several at the same instant, on separate cores or machines. | One core can handle thousands of concurrent requests if they spend most of their time waiting. Parallelism only helps work that's actually computing.                                                                                  | Asynchronous I/O                                                            |

### 8. Doing less work per request

| id                        | Concept                               | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                         | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Often confused with                                                                         |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `non-blocking-io`         | Asynchronous (non-blocking) I/O       | 2    | any      | Instead of a thread sitting idle while it waits on a database or network call, the server parks the request and uses the thread for other work until the reply arrives (event loops, async/await). | Most request time is spent waiting. A thread-per-request server runs out of threads while the CPU sits idle; a non-blocking server serves thousands of waiting requests with a few threads. CPU-heavy work gets no faster.                                          | Parallelism; background jobs                                                                |
| `connection-pooling`      | Connection pooling                    | 2    | any      | Keep a set of open connections to a database or service and reuse them, instead of opening a new one for every request.                                                                            | Opening a connection costs round trips for TCP, TLS and login, and reusing one costs almost nothing. The pool size also caps load on the database. Scaling out multiplies it: 100 instances × 20 connections is 2,000, which is why pooling proxies sit in between. | Thread pools; caching                                                                       |
| `batching`                | Batching (and chatty I/O)             | 2    | any      | Combine many small operations into one call: one insert of 500 rows, one request for 50 items.                                                                                                     | Every call pays a fixed overhead, such as a round trip or a commit. A chatty pattern of many tiny calls pays it over and over. Batching spreads the overhead across many items, raising throughput for a little extra latency.                                      | Request coalescing; N+1 queries                                                             |
| `compression`             | Compression                           | 2    | any      | Shrink data before sending or storing it (gzip, Brotli, zstd).                                                                                                                                     | Text and JSON often shrink by 70–90%, so transfers are faster on slow links and egress is cheaper, for some CPU. Images and video are already compressed and gain nothing.                                                                                          | Minification                                                                                |
| `pagination`              | Pagination (fetch only what you need) | 2    | any      | Return a large result one page at a time, with only the fields that were asked for.                                                                                                                | Fetching everything to show ten rows wastes the database, the network and memory. Offset pages get slower the deeper you go, because the skipped rows are still read; cursor (keyset) pages stay fast.                                                              | Streaming; infinite scroll (a UI pattern)                                                   |
| `n-plus-one-queries`      | N+1 queries                           | 2    | any      | Code fetches a list with one query, then runs another query for each item's details: 1 + N round trips.                                                                                            | It looks fine with 10 rows in testing and falls over with 10,000 in production. A join or one batched query (WHERE id IN …) does the same work in one or two round trips. ORM lazy loading is the usual culprit.                                                    | Chatty I/O (the general form)                                                               |
| `materialised-view`       | Materialised views and precomputation | 2    | any      | Compute expensive results (joins, totals, feeds) ahead of time, either when data is written or on a schedule, and store them ready to read.                                                        | Move work from read time to write time. If something is read a thousand times for every change, computing it once per change is far cheaper. Reads become lookups, at the cost of storage and slightly stale results.                                               | Cache (filled lazily on reads; a view is kept up to date in advance); denormalisation; CQRS |
| `scaling-reads-vs-writes` | Scaling reads vs scaling writes       | 2    | any      | Reads and writes scale differently. Reads scale by adding copies (caches, read replicas); writes scale by splitting the data (sharding) or smoothing them out (queues).                            | Copies multiply read capacity, but every copy must apply every write, so copies don't help writes. To scale writes, each node must own only part of the data.                                                                                                       | Read replicas and sharding (the techniques it points to, owned by Data)                     |

### 9. Protecting capacity

| id                     | Concept                                  | Tier | Platform | What it is                                                                                                                                                                            | The idea to get across                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Often confused with                                                                                    |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `overload`             | Overload and goodput collapse            | 2    | any      | What happens past capacity: queues grow, latency passes clients' timeouts, servers work on requests whose clients have given up, and retries add even more load.                      | Throughput doesn't level off at the limit. Useful throughput (goodput) collapses while the servers show 100% busy. Overload has to be stopped at the door, not absorbed.                                                                    | High load (busy but coping)                                                                            |
| `rate-limiting`        | Rate limiting                            | 1    | any      | Cap how many requests each client (API key, user or IP) may make in a time window. Requests over the limit get HTTP 429 Too Many Requests.                                            | No single client can use up capacity meant for everyone, so usage stays fair and predictable and abuse is blunted. With many gateway instances, counters have to be shared (e.g. in Redis), or each instance enforces a slice of the limit. | Throttling; quotas                                                                                     |
| `token-bucket`         | Token bucket                             | 2    | any      | Tokens drip into a bucket at a steady rate, up to a maximum. Each request spends one token, and a request that finds no token is rejected.                                            | Two numbers make two promises: the refill rate is the sustained limit, and the bucket size is the burst allowed. Short bursts get through; sustained overuse doesn't.                                                                       | Leaky bucket                                                                                           |
| `leaky-bucket`         | Leaky bucket                             | 3    | any      | Requests join a queue that drains at a fixed rate. When the queue is full, new requests are dropped.                                                                                  | Whatever shape traffic arrives in, it leaves as a smooth, steady stream, which suits feeding a fragile downstream. Bursts are delayed or dropped, never passed straight through.                                                            | Token bucket (which lets bursts through)                                                               |
| `window-rate-limiting` | Fixed and sliding window limits          | 2    | any      | Count requests per time window. A fixed window resets on the minute; a sliding window always counts the last 60 seconds.                                                              | A fixed window lets through double the limit around a boundary: a full quota at 0:59 and another at 1:00. A sliding window smooths this out, for a little more memory or arithmetic.                                                        | Token bucket                                                                                           |
| `throttling`           | Throttling and backing off on 429s       | 2    | any      | A busy service slows or rejects callers (429 or 503 with Retry-After) instead of falling over, and well-behaved callers pace themselves to stay within the limits they've been given. | Throttling is a two-way conversation: the server signals "too much", and the client waits as told, then retries with backoff. Clients that ignore the signal turn throttling into a retry storm.                                            | Rate limiting (a fixed cap per client); load shedding                                                  |
| `quotas`               | Quotas                                   | 2    | any      | A cap on total usage over a longer period, or on a resource: 10,000 API calls a day, 100 vCPUs per region.                                                                            | Rate limits cap speed; quotas cap amount. Cloud quotas are a hidden scaling limit, because autoscaling stops when it reaches the quota. Check and raise them before the peak.                                                               | Rate limiting                                                                                          |
| `load-shedding`        | Load shedding                            | 2    | any      | Close to capacity, deliberately reject some requests quickly and cheaply (e.g. with a 503) so the rest are served well, dropping the least important first.                           | Serving 90% of requests fast beats serving 100% too slowly for anyone. Rejecting early costs almost nothing compared with timing out after the work is done.                                                                                | Rate limiting (a fixed cap per client; shedding reacts to the server's own load); graceful degradation |
| `backpressure`         | Backpressure                             | 2    | any      | A slow consumer signals its producers to slow down, through bounded buffers that fill up and block, credits, or explicit "stop" responses.                                            | Without backpressure, the gap between a fast producer and a slow consumer piles up in unbounded queues and memory until something crashes. With it, the slowdown travels back to the source, where it can be handled.                       | Load shedding (which drops work; backpressure slows the sender)                                        |
| `queue-load-levelling` | Queue-based load levelling (**shipped**) | 2    | any      | Put a queue between the callers and the workers. A burst waits in the queue while the workers process at their own pace and scale out.                                                | Workers are sized for the average, not the peak, because the queue absorbs the spike as a backlog. It works when callers can wait for the result.                                                                                           | Backpressure; load balancing                                                                           |
| `admission-control`    | Admission control and concurrency limits | 3    | any      | Decide at the door whether to accept each request, based on how much work is already in progress. Adaptive limits adjust the cap from observed latency.                               | Limit concurrency, not requests per second. A cap on in-flight work keeps latency stable whatever each request costs, and turning the overflow away protects everything already admitted.                                                   | Rate limiting; load shedding                                                                           |
| `noisy-neighbour`      | Noisy neighbour                          | 2    | any      | One tenant, customer or job uses so much of a shared resource that everyone else on it slows down.                                                                                    | Shared capacity needs limits per tenant (quotas, rate limits, separate pools or partitions), so that one customer's spike stays their own problem.                                                                                          | General overload                                                                                       |

### Other sources (SRE load-balancing chapters, SRE Workbook, AWS Builders' Library)

- **Load balancing in the datacenter.** Round robin, least-loaded and weighted round robin are in
  Group 3, and the lame duck state is `connection-draining`. Subsetting (each client only talking to
  a subset of backends) is **left out**: it's a Google-scale detail of client-side load balancing.
- **Load balancing at the frontend.** DNS load balancing and anycast are partly
  `global-load-balancing` and mostly Networking; VIPs, connection tracking, direct server return and
  packet encapsulation belong to Networking; Maglev's consistent hashing is `consistent-hashing`.
- **SRE Workbook, Managing Load.** GSLB is `global-load-balancing`. The autoscaling pitfalls
  (unhealthy instances counted in averages, stateful systems, scaling conservatively, limits and a
  kill switch, downstream dependencies) are covered by `autoscaling`, `scaling-metrics`,
  `sticky-sessions` and `bottleneck`. Combining shedding with balancing is `load-shedding`.
- **Builders' Library, caching.** Local vs external caches is `distributed-cache`; cold starts and
  modal behaviour (a service hooked on its cache) are `cold-cache`; thundering herd is
  `cache-stampede`; then `request-coalescing` and `negative-caching`; soft vs hard TTL is
  `stale-while-revalidate`; sizing is `cache-hit-ratio`; eviction is `cache-eviction`.
- **Builders' Library, load shedding.** Goodput and wasted work are `overload`; shedding is
  `load-shedding`.
